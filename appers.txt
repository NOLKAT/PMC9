PHYSIOLOGICAL MODELING CHALLENGE 9 — PAPERS FOR PARTS 1-3
======================================================================

This file maps each implemented part to the papers supporting its model
parameters. Values described as "selected" or "calibrated" are modeling
choices informed by the cited range, not direct measurements from one paper.


PART 1 — FASTING BASELINE
-------------------------

1. Dressman JB, Berardi RR, Dermentzoglou LC, et al. Upper gastrointestinal
   (GI) pH in young, healthy men and women. Pharmaceutical Research.
   1990;7(7):756-761. DOI: 10.1023/A:1015827908309.
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2395805/

   Association with the model:
   - Median fasting gastric pH = 1.7.
   - After a standard meal, median gastric pH briefly rose to 6.7 and returned
     toward the fasting value in less than two hours.

2. Grimm M, Scholz E, Koziolek M, Kühn JP, Weitschies W. Interindividual and
   intraindividual variability of fasted state gastric fluid volume and
   gastric emptying of water. European Journal of Pharmaceutics and
   Biopharmaceutics. 2018;127:309-317.
   DOI: 10.1016/j.ejpb.2018.03.002.
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29522898/

   Association with the model:
   - Mean resting gastric fluid volume after an overnight fast = 25 +/- 18 mL.
   - The model uses 25 mL as its initial fasting fluid volume.

3. Feldman M, Richardson CT, Lam SK, Samloff IM. Comparison of gastric acid
   secretion rates and serum gastrin concentrations in duodenal ulcer patients
   and normal subjects. Journal of Clinical Investigation.
   1987;79(1):294-298. DOI: 10.1172/JCI112850.
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC424132/

   Association with the model:
   - A mean basal acid secretion rate of about 3.3 mmol/h was reported for the
     normal-subject group.
   - The model uses 3.3 mmol/h for basal secretion.


PART 2 — UNTREATED HEAVY MEAL
------------------------------

1. Fletcher J, Wirz A, Henry E, McColl KEL. Studies of regional postprandial
   differences in pH within the stomach and gastroesophageal junction.
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16416175/

   Association with the model:
   - A high-volume fatty dinner raised proximal gastric pH to about 4.9 and
     mid/distal gastric pH to about 4.0.
   - Its buffering effect kept pH above 4 for approximately 150 minutes.
   - The model therefore uses a meal-buffered pH ceiling of 5.0 and targets a
     prolonged heavy-meal buffering period. The empirical titration exponent
     is calibrated to reproduce this duration; it is not a chemical pKa.

2. Gardner JD, Ciociola AA, Robinson M. Measurement of meal-stimulated gastric
   acid secretion by in vivo gastric autotitration. Journal of Applied
   Physiology. 2002;92(2):427-434. DOI: 10.1152/japplphysiol.00956.2001.
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11796648/

   Association with the model:
   - The standard solid meal required 119 mmol HCl for titration to pH 2.
   - Median meal-stimulated secretion was 60 mmol/h, with an interquartile
     range of 40-71 mmol/h.
   - The model uses the measured 119 mmol buffer capacity and a selected peak
     secretion rate of 40 mmol/h.

3. Walsh JH, Richardson CT, Fordtran JS. Variable contribution of gastrin to
   gastric acid secretion after a meal in humans. Gastroenterology.
   1987;92(1):179-184.
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3557000/

   Association with the model:
   - Meal-stimulated acid secretion averaged 24.2 +/- 2.4 mmol/h.
   - Together with Gardner et al.'s 60 mmol/h median, this supports selecting
     40 mmol/h as an intermediate heavy-meal peak rather than claiming it is
     a universal measured value.

4. Richardson CT, Walsh JH, Cooper KA, Feldman M, Fordtran JS. Studies on the
   mechanisms of food-stimulated gastric acid secretion in normal human
   subjects. Journal of Clinical Investigation. 1976;58(3):623-631.
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/956391/

   Association with the model:
   - The study used 500- and 750-mL meals and showed that gastric distension is
     an important stimulus for acid secretion.
   - The challenge model uses 750 mL to represent a heavy meal; 500 mL is the
     natural comparison for a more typical experimental meal.

5. Marciani L, Cox EF, Hoad CL, et al. Additive effects of gastric volumes and
   macronutrient composition on the sensation of postprandial fullness in
   humans. European Journal of Clinical Nutrition. 2015;69:380-384.
   DOI: 10.1038/ejcn.2014.194.
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4351404/

   Association with the model:
   - Approximately 500-mL MRI meals had half-emptying times of 99 +/- 8 and
     122 +/- 10 minutes and returned near baseline volume within about 3 hours.
   - The model uses a slower 150-minute half-time as an explicit extrapolation
     for a larger 750-mL heavy meal. This is a modeling assumption, not a value
     directly measured in this paper.


PART 3 — WHAT OMEPRAZOLE IS AND HOW ITS EFFECT CHANGES WITH TIME
----------------------------------------------------------------

1. Shin JM, Sachs G. Pharmacology of proton pump inhibitors. Current
   Gastroenterology Reports. 2008;10(6):528-534.
   DOI: 10.1007/s11894-008-0098-4.
   https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2855237/

   Association with the model:
   - Omeprazole is an acid-activated prodrug that covalently inhibits the
     parietal-cell H+/K+-ATPase.
   - The plasma half-life is about one hour, but inhibition persists because
     the pump is covalently modified.
   - Steady-state inhibition requires about 2-3 days of repeated dosing.
   - Optimized once-daily dosing was estimated to inhibit about 66% of maximal
     acid output; the model uses 66% as its asymptote.
   - The review reports an estimated human recovery half-time of approximately
     24 hours after omeprazole inhibition; the model uses 24 hours.

   Suitability note:
   This is a strong review for the mechanism and overall model structure. The
   66% value is a calculated estimate, and several kinetic values summarize
   earlier studies, so the primary PK/PD paper below is included as support.

2. Katashima M, Yamamoto K, Tokuma Y, Hata T, Sawada Y, Iga T. Comparative
   pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic analysis of proton pump inhibitors
   omeprazole, lansoprazole and pantoprazole in humans. European Journal of
   Drug Metabolism and Pharmacokinetics. 1998;23(1):19-26.
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9625268/

   Association with the model:
   - The apparent turnover rate constant for the omeprazole effect corresponded
     to an inhibitory-effect half-life below approximately 27.5 hours.
   - This supports using a roughly 24-hour recovery half-time instead of making
     omeprazole switch off immediately when plasma drug disappears.

3. Hürlimann S, Abbühl B, Inauen W, Halter F. Comparison of acid inhibition by
   either oral high-dose ranitidine or omeprazole. Alimentary Pharmacology &
   Therapeutics. 1994;8(2):193-201. DOI: 10.1111/j.1365-2036.1994.tb00278.x.
   https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8038351/

   Association with the model:
   - Omeprazole inhibition increased over the first treatment week, unlike the
     rapid first-day peak observed with the H2-receptor antagonist ranitidine.
   - This supports showing a slow multiday onset in Part 3.


MODEL CAUTIONS
--------------

- The untreated meal model is a one-compartment teaching model. Real gastric
  pH varies by location, and an acidic proximal pocket may coexist with more
  strongly buffered distal contents.
- The pH conversion before buffer exhaustion is an empirical titration curve,
  not a full chemical buffer model with measured pKa values. Its exponent is
  calibrated against the approximately 150-minute pH-above-4 observation.
- Secreted acid first consumes the remaining food-buffer capacity. Only acid
  produced after that capacity is exhausted enters the free-acid balance.
- The secretion and emptying time constants should be included in a later
  sensitivity analysis because they are calibrated assumptions.
- Part 3 describes omeprazole and its pump-level time course. The treated heavy
  meal belongs to Part 4 and is intentionally not implemented yet.
